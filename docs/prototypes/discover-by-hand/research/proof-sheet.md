# Proof Sheet: research

**Process behind the look:** Proofs date from about 1600. Galleys were metal trays of hand-set type, inked on a small proof press so authors and editors could mark errors before the type was fixed. Proofs were printed with extra-wide margins on purpose, to leave room for notes. Each correction has two parts: a mark in the text where the fault is, and a matching mark in the margin saying what to do. The marginal mark is the instruction the typesetter reads; the textual mark only locates it. Standards (BS 5261, ISO 5776, Chicago) exist so a typesetter who may not read the language can still follow them. Hand-written means the marks vary in angle and pressure. Background knowledge, not from the sources read: convention often used one colour for printer's errors and another for author or editor changes.

**Colour, texture, composition:** Printed text is one dark ink on uncoated stock, so the sheet is effectively two colours: black type plus the marker's pen or pencil. The text block stays orderly and the margins carry the mess. Marks sit on top of the type and cross it, with no fills. Lines are thin and uneven, in a dozen small gestures on a dense page, not a few large ones.

**Rules:**
1. The text column is set cleanly and never decorated; all markup lives in the margins or sits on top of it.
2. Every mark has a textual half and a marginal half, joined by position (a caret in the line, its instruction beside it).
3. Marks are strokes only, never filled shapes and never boxes with backgrounds.
4. One marking colour, used only for correction marks. Nothing else is that colour.
5. Marginal notes are terse: an abbreviation or a word (stet, tr, ital, lc), not a sentence.
6. Wide margins are the point. Wherever the text is full-bleed, the proof reading is lost.
7. A mark means something. Strike = cut, caret = add, circle = resolve or pick, stet = reverse the change. No decorative marks.
8. Hand-drawn wobble is small, and varies between marks (angle, length, pressure); identical repeated strokes read as a stamp.
9. Marks may cross type but never make it unreadable; the struck text stays legible.
10. Stet is the only mark that undoes another. Use it once, for the ending.

**Named patterns:**
- Dele (deleatur): delete; a looped tail in the margin.
- Caret: insert here; the inserted text goes in the margin.
- Pilcrow ¶: new paragraph.
- Close up: tie two parts together.
- Stet: let it stand; dotted underline plus "stet" in the margin.
- Transpose (tr), Lower case (lc), Italic (ital), Small caps (sc), Flush left (fl).
- Underline conventions: single is italic, wavy is bold, double is small caps, triple is capitals, dashed is stet.
- Circled punctuation: a mark inside a circle means insert that mark.
- Slash separator: "/" ends each marginal instruction, so several can share one margin.
- Blue pencil: the editing pencil, as a historical term.
- Uncorrected proof / galley / final proof: the stamps and labels on the sheet.

**Where this Direction risks a cliché:** "Warm paper plus red pen" is the default skin for anything about editing, and grease-pencil contact sheets, route lines and highlighter strokes are all the same cosplay. All four seeds use serif display plus a mono label. Real proofs have no ochre highlighter, no drawn route line and no film-frame thumbnails; they are dense, plain and a little ugly. Marks only feel earned if they carry real meaning (rule 7).
